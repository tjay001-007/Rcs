 ACFT11_a_121 FLIGHT CONTROL SYSTEM
 Controller Structure — Descriptive (textbook / systems-engineering style)
================================================================================


1.  DESIGN PHILOSOPHY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The flight control system is organised as a layered, cascaded controller. Each
layer has one clearly defined job and talks only to the layer directly above and
below it. From the outside in, the layers move from "slow, strategic" decisions
to "fast, physical" actions:

    - the outer layers decide WHERE the aircraft should go,
    - the middle layers decide WHAT ATTITUDE achieves that,
    - the inner layer decides WHICH SURFACE MOVEMENTS produce that attitude.

This separation is deliberate. It means a single robust inner loop can handle all
the difficult, fast, nonlinear airframe behaviour, while the slower outer loops
stay simple and can be tuned with constant gains that work across the whole
flight envelope (from takeoff at low speed to Mach 4 and back to landing).


2.  FUNCTIONAL BLOCKS (what each block does, in plain terms)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BLOCK 1 — MISSION SEQUENCER  (the "brain" / mode manager)
    A state machine that knows the whole mission as a list of 17 phases
    (ground roll, rotation, lift-off, climb, cruise, accelerate, zoom climb,
    stage separation, coast, glide, engine relight, return-to-base, descent,
    approach, flare, touchdown, roll-out).
    Its job: watch the aircraft's speed, altitude, Mach number and position, and
    decide WHICH PHASE the aircraft is currently in. It then tells the rest of
    the system three things: which guidance goal to pursue, which control law
    should be in charge of pitch and throttle, and which engines should be
    running.

BLOCK 2 — GUIDANCE  (the "navigator")
    Takes the current phase from the sequencer and turns it into concrete
    targets: a desired flight-path angle, a desired airspeed, a desired altitude
    and a desired heading. For example, in the climb it asks for a climb speed
    and altitude; in the glide it points the nose toward home and manages energy;
    on approach it follows an ILS-style glide slope to the runway.
    Its job: convert "what phase am I in" into "what path and speed do I want".

BLOCK 3 — OUTER CONTROL LAWS  (the "pilot's hands and feet", four laws)
    These convert the guidance targets into ATTITUDE and RATE commands:
      - TECS (Total Energy Control): manages airspeed and altitude together by
        treating them as energy. It decides how much throttle to add and how much
        to pitch up or down, so speed and altitude never fight each other.
      - ACAH (Attitude Command / Attitude Hold): compares the desired pitch and
        bank angle with the actual angle and asks for a turn rate to close the
        gap.
      - Gamma law: in the zoom, coast and glide, it commands pitch directly from
        flight-path-angle error (used when flying a trajectory rather than an
        altitude).
      - L1 lateral guidance: steers the aircraft onto its track by asking for a
        bank angle, which then becomes a roll-rate command.
    Their job: turn "where I want to go" into "what attitude/rate I need".

BLOCK 4 — ENVELOPE PROTECTION  (the "safety fence")
    Sits just before the inner loop and clamps every command so the aircraft
    cannot be asked to do something unsafe: it limits angle of attack, limits the
    normal load factor (g), and limits bank angle. No matter what the guidance or
    the pilot asks for, the aircraft stays inside its structural and aerodynamic
    limits.
    Its job: never let the commands exceed safe limits.

BLOCK 5 — INDI INNER LOOP  (the "muscle controller", runs 100 times a second)
    This is the heart of the controller. It receives the desired roll/pitch/yaw
    rates and works out exactly how far to move the elevator, ailerons and rudder
    to achieve them. It does this incrementally: it measures how fast the aircraft
    is actually rotating right now, compares that with what is wanted, and nudges
    the surfaces by just the right amount to make up the difference.
    Because it works from live measurements, it does not need a perfect
    mathematical model of the aircraft and automatically copes with changes in
    speed, altitude and weight.
    Its job: turn rate commands into precise control-surface movements.

BLOCK 6 — ACTUATORS  (the moving surfaces)
    The elevator, ailerons and rudder. They have real limits on how far and how
    fast they can move; the inner loop respects those limits so the surfaces
    never slam or flutter.

BLOCK 7 — REACTION CONTROL SYSTEM (RCS)  (attitude control in thin air)
    High above the atmosphere the air is too thin for the control surfaces to
    work. There, small thrusters hold the aircraft's attitude instead. The system
    blends smoothly between surfaces and thrusters based on dynamic pressure (how
    much "bite" the air has): surfaces fade in as speed/air density build, RCS
    fades out.
    Its job: keep attitude controllable when the surfaces cannot.

BLOCK 8 — PROPULSION MANAGER  (the engines)
    Runs the right engines for each phase (dry jet, afterburner, rocket aerospike,
    or idle) as commanded by the sequencer, and builds thrust up smoothly rather
    than instantly (a realistic engine spool-up).
    Its job: provide thrust appropriate to the phase.

BLOCK 9 — PLANT / EQUATIONS OF MOTION  (the aircraft itself)
    The aerodynamics, propulsion and landing gear produce forces and moments on
    the airframe; the 6-degree-of-freedom equations of motion integrate those to
    give the aircraft's new position, velocity and attitude. This is the physics
    the controller is trying to steer.

BLOCK 10 — SENSORS / FEEDBACK  (the aircraft's awareness)
    The current speed, altitude, Mach, angle of attack, attitude, rates, dynamic
    pressure and weight-on-wheels are measured and fed back to every block above,
    so each layer always acts on the true state of the aircraft.


3.  HOW THE BLOCKS CONNECT (signal flow, in words)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    The SEQUENCER decides the phase and hands goals to GUIDANCE.
    GUIDANCE produces target path, speed, altitude and heading.
    The OUTER LAWS turn those targets into attitude and rate commands
        (and TECS also sets the throttle).
    ENVELOPE PROTECTION clamps those commands to safe limits.
    The INDI INNER LOOP turns the safe rate commands into surface movements.
    The ACTUATORS (and RCS in thin air) move, creating aerodynamic and thrust
        forces on the PLANT.
    The PLANT (equations of motion) produces the new aircraft state.
    The SENSORS feed that state back up to every block, closing all the loops.


4.  DECISION FLOW (how the sequencer advances the mission)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
This is the systems-engineering "decision flow" — plain if/then logic. Each step
is checked continuously; when a condition is met, the sequencer advances.

    START: on the runway, engines idle.

    [1] GROUND ROLL
        IF airspeed >= rotation speed  -> go to ROTATION.

    [2] ROTATION
        Raise the nose to lift-off pitch.
        IF the wheels leave the ground (low gear load)  -> go to LIFT-OFF.

    [3] LIFT-OFF
        Hold a fixed climb pitch, full thrust.
        IF altitude above the gear-safe height  -> go to CLIMB.

    [4] CLIMB
        Climb at the climb speed toward cruise altitude (TECS in charge).
        IF cruise altitude reached  -> go to CRUISE.

    [5] CRUISE
        Hold altitude and speed.
        IF the cruise dwell time has elapsed (boost armed)  -> go to ACCELERATE.

    [6] ACCELERATE
        Add afterburner and build Mach; gently pre-pitch the nose up as speed
        builds (this keeps the later pull-up gentle).
        IF Mach reaches the transition Mach  -> go to ZOOM.

    [7] ZOOM CLIMB
        Light the rocket (aerospike), pull up to the zoom flight-path angle, and
        climb hard. The pull is rate-limited so the g stays below 2.5.
        IF apogee altitude reached, or the rocket burn time ends  -> go to
        SEPARATION.

    [8] STAGE SEPARATION
        Let the rocket thrust decay over about a second (not a sudden cut), drop
        the second-stage mass smoothly, and hold a small positive angle of attack
        along the arc.
        After a short settle time  -> go to COAST.

    [9] COAST (thin air, near-ballistic)
        Follow the flight path with a small nose-up bias; hold attitude with the
        RCS thrusters because the air is too thin for the surfaces.
        IF dynamic pressure rises back to a useful level (descending into denser
        air)  -> go to GLIDE.

    [10] GLIDE
        Point toward home and manage energy: glide shallow when far, steeper when
        high or close. Surfaces are back in control.
        IF inside the engine-relight window (right Mach and dynamic pressure)
        -> go to RELIGHT.

    [11] ENGINE RELIGHT
        Spool the air-breathing engine back up smoothly.
        After the spool-up time  -> go to RETURN-TO-BASE CRUISE.

    [12] RETURN-TO-BASE
        Cruise home.
        IF within capture range of home  -> go to DESCENT.

    [13] DESCENT
        Descend to the approach platform altitude.
        IF the glide-slope beam is intercepted  -> go to APPROACH.

    [14] APPROACH
        Follow the localizer and 3-degree glide slope down to the runway, easing
        to approach speed.
        IF at flare height above the ground  -> go to FLARE.

    [15] FLARE
        Cut thrust to idle and gently raise the nose to arrest the sink rate for a
        soft touchdown.
        IF wheels on the ground  -> go to TOUCHDOWN / ROLL-OUT.

    [16] ROLL-OUT
        Lower the nose, apply braking.
        IF speed below stop speed  -> STOPPED.

    END: stopped on the runway.


5.  KEY IDEAS TO REMEMBER
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    - LAYERS: strategy on the outside (slow), physics on the inside (fast).
    - ONE ROBUST INNER LOOP (INDI) absorbs all the hard nonlinear behaviour, so
      the outer loops stay simple.
    - DYNAMIC PRESSURE is the master "gatekeeper": it decides how much authority
      the surfaces have, and when to hand attitude control to the RCS thrusters.
    - SAFETY FIRST: the envelope-protection fence sits between the commands and
      the surfaces and can override anything.
    - THE SEQUENCER is a decision flow: each phase has a simple entry condition,
      and the mission advances automatically as conditions are met.
